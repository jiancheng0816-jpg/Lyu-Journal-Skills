# Information-design literature positioning

Use this protocol when the paper involves forecasting, information acquisition, information sharing, cheap talk, signaling, screening, disclosure, recommendation, Bayesian persuasion, or data precision. These literatures may share vocabulary while studying different information stages and commitment structures.

## 1. Map the information lifecycle

For the focal paper and each close neighbor, record:

| Stage | Comparison question |
| --- | --- |
| Availability | Does relevant information already exist? |
| Acquisition | Who can generate or purchase information and at what cost? |
| State knowledge | Who observes the state, forecast, or signal accuracy? |
| Communication | Is the message truthful, cheap talk, verifiable, or strategically distorted? |
| Commitment | Can an actor commit ex ante to an experiment or disclosure rule? |
| Experiment set | Is arbitrary Bayes-plausible persuasion feasible or is testing technologically restricted? |
| Disclosure | Is full, partial, selective, or no disclosure endogenous? |
| Receiver action | Which operational decision changes after the message? |
| Other levers | Are price, contract, subsidy, capacity, commission, or targeting jointly chosen? |
| Outcome | Which action, profit, welfare, or efficiency object is characterized? |

Do not position a persuasion paper merely as “information sharing with endogenous transparency.” Explain the commitment and experiment-design difference.

## 2. Classify neighboring mechanism families

Useful families include:

- forecasting or statistical learning from historical data;
- costly information acquisition or experimentation;
- truthful information sharing with exogenous signals;
- cheap talk without commitment;
- signaling through costly endogenous actions;
- screening through contracts or menus;
- Bayesian persuasion through ex ante experiment commitment;
- recommendation or disclosure with restricted experiments;
- joint information and monetary or operational design.

A paper may connect several families. Identify the boundary between them and which linkage generates the focal result.

## 3. Compare operational geometry, not only the solution method

Two papers may both use Bayesian persuasion but remain structurally different. Compare:

- state variable: mean, variance, tail risk, quality, congestion, type, or forecast accuracy;
- receiver action and feasible set;
- sender and receiver preferred actions as posteriors change;
- curvature of the sender interim payoff;
- posterior regions of preference alignment or misalignment;
- experiment restrictions and signal support;
- joint monetary or operational levers;
- benchmark and stakeholder outcomes.

The contribution is not “we also apply persuasion.” It must arise from a changed operational response, preference geometry, feasible experiment, lever interaction, or stakeholder trade-off.

## 4. Position joint-lever contributions

When information is optimized with price, subsidy, contract, commission, capacity, or another lever, search for four neighbor types:

1. information design with the noninformation lever fixed;
2. the noninformation lever without information design;
3. joint design in another operational setting;
4. the same operational decision under another information mechanism.

State whether the focal paper adds:

- a new endogenous lever;
- a new allocation of decision rights;
- substitution-to-complementarity reversal;
- an inactive-lever region;
- a new preference-alignment region;
- a new stakeholder or efficiency consequence.

## 5. Verify formal-result provenance

For every lemma, theorem, or characterization drawn from a close paper, record:

| Focal result | Source result | Exact/specialized/adapted | Conditions matched? | New downstream use | Contribution status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

Use explicit attribution in the manuscript. An inherited receiver best response or standard persuasion characterization may be essential to the paper without being a contribution.

## 6. Write the literature architecture

A useful information-design review often moves through analytical stages rather than applications:

1. how information is generated or forecast;
2. how credibility and strategic communication are handled;
3. how commitment enables experiment or disclosure design;
4. how information interacts with the focal operational or monetary lever;
5. the closest paper and the changed preference geometry or decision problem.

This is a routing option, not a mandatory number of paragraphs. Preserve the smallest stream architecture that makes the contribution visible.

## 7. Calibrate contribution language

Prefer:

- “endogenizes the experiment while jointly choosing…”;
- “shows how the receiver's posterior response changes the optimal disclosure…”;
- “identifies when information and pricing act as substitutes or complements…”;
- “distinguishes information generation from strategic disclosure…”;
- “changes the sender-receiver preference alignment by…”.

Avoid claiming that forecasting, sharing, disclosure, and persuasion are interchangeable or that an application is unexplored without a reproducible search.
