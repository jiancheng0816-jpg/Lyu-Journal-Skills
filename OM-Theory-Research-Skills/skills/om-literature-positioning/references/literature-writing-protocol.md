# Literature and contribution writing protocol for analytical OM papers

Use this protocol only after the candidate ledger and closest-paper matrix have been verified. It converts evidence into manuscript prose without turning the literature review into an annotated bibliography.

## 1. Select streams by analytical role

Use two to four streams that perform distinct intellectual jobs, such as:

- the theoretical foundation for the focal mechanism;
- operational decisions or contracts in the focal institution;
- downstream markets, continuation values, information systems, or strategic interactions linked by the model.

Do not create a stream merely because several papers share an application keyword. A valid stream has a central decision problem and a coherent mechanism family.

## 2. Write each stream as an argument

Each stream should normally contain these moves:

1. State the stream's central question or decision problem.
2. Synthesize papers into mechanism or decision clusters.
3. Identify the closest paper or small cluster and what it endogenizes.
4. State the focal paper's exact difference in primitives, timing, information, decisions, or constraints.
5. Explain the changed payoff, active constraint, or strategic response.
6. Name the verified result that follows from that mechanism difference.

The final two moves are essential. “We study a different setting” or “we consider an additional stage” is not a mechanism-level contribution unless the added feature changes an incentive or equilibrium object.

When policy design is central, compare mandatory versus voluntary adoption, who chooses policy intensity, which instruments become available, and which stakeholder outcomes change. A transfer of decision rights can be the structural difference even when primitives are otherwise identical.

When information is central, distinguish whether each stream studies availability, acquisition, forecasting, truthful sharing, cheap talk, signaling, screening, persuasion, or restricted disclosure. Compare commitment, experiment set, receiver action, and the interaction with monetary or operational levers. Read [information-design-positioning.md](information-design-positioning.md) for this mode.

## 3. Recover or distinguish the closest model

For the strongest structural neighbor, determine whether the focal model nests it.

If nested:

1. State the exact parameter, policy, or decision restriction.
2. Verify that payoffs, feasible sets, information, and equilibrium concept coincide.
3. Recover the closest paper's result.
4. Explain which relaxed restriction creates the new mechanism or result region.

If nonnested, state the strongest overlap and the consequential difference, but do not use `generalizes`, `subsumes`, or “special case.” A benchmark comparison is still useful without a nesting claim.

## 4. Handle superficially similar results

When the focal paper and a closest paper produce the same observable action, compare why the action occurs. Useful questions include:

- Which agent bears the cost?
- Which participation, incentive, feasibility, or continuation-value constraint binds?
- Is one instrument independently distorted while another moves mechanically?
- Does the result arise from consumer behavior, firm continuation value, platform design, or equilibrium selection?
- Does the mechanism survive when the focal feature is removed?

State the shared outcome first, then the distinct mechanism and its additional equilibrium implication. Do not claim novelty merely because notation or the application differs.

## 5. Write result-level agreement and reversal comparisons

Use literature comparisons inside the analysis only when they sharpen the interpretation of a formal result. For each such comparison:

1. identify the focal proposition or corollary and the precise directional, regime, or policy conclusion;
2. state whether the closest paper obtains a consistent, opposite, or conditional version of that conclusion;
3. compare the active constraint, marginal trade-off, outside option, decision owner, information friction, or equilibrium channel;
4. explain why the mechanisms generate agreement or reversal;
5. state the additional observable or managerial implication that distinguishes the focal result.

Do not attach a citation to every proposition mechanically. Omit the comparison when no verified close analogue exists, and never infer agreement from similar labels alone. If two results have the same sign but different mechanisms, say so. If they have opposite signs, identify the parameter region and changed mechanism rather than describing the finding as merely surprising.

## 6. Cross-stream synthesis

End the literature review with one paragraph that explains:

1. what each stream explains separately;
2. which linkage the focal model introduces;
3. why that linkage changes the joint decision or equilibrium;
4. which headline result becomes visible only through the integration.

Avoid broad claims that entire literatures study topics “in isolation” unless the search record supports them. Prefer search-bounded and mechanism-specific wording.

## 7. Introduction contribution paragraphs

Align contribution paragraphs with the introduction's two to four finding groups, not necessarily one paragraph per literature stream. For each contribution:

1. name the closest conversation or paper;
2. acknowledge the strongest overlap;
3. state the structural difference;
4. explain the mechanism consequence;
5. state the verified result and boundary;
6. explain the decision question newly answered.

When valid, use benchmark nesting to strengthen the paragraph: first recover the closest result, then state the parameter region, new instrument, or decision-right allocation that changes it.

Use result verbs carefully:

- `characterizes` for an equilibrium or policy map;
- `identifies` for a regime, threshold, or mechanism;
- `shows` for a proved comparative static or existence result;
- `demonstrates robustness` only when the extension covers the stated class;
- `suggests` for bounded managerial interpretation.

Attribute inherited, specialized, and adapted formal results directly. The contribution paragraph should begin where the focal paper's new operational response, experiment, lever interaction, or stakeholder result begins, not at a reused receiver best response.

## 8. Evidence and language controls

- Preserve `FULL TEXT CHECKED`, `ABSTRACT CHECKED`, and `METADATA ONLY` limits when drafting.
- Do not attribute a model assumption, result, or limitation without a precise source locator.
- Use “differs from,” “extends,” “endogenizes,” “links,” and “complements” when supported.
- Use “first,” “unexplored,” “unique,” or “the literature ignores” only with documented coverage sufficient for that claim.
- Do not use prestige, publication recency, or citation count as a substitute for structural proximity.
- Keep institutional evidence separate from contribution evidence.

## 9. Handoff test

Before approving prose, verify that the literature review and introduction use the same:

- focal decisions and mechanism labels;
- closest-paper set;
- structural differences;
- headline result grouping;
- boundary conditions and evidence status.
- nesting restriction or explicit nonnesting status;
- policy, instrument, decision-owner, and stakeholder terminology when relevant.
- information-lifecycle stage, commitment structure, experiment set, receiver action, informativeness language, and formal-result provenance when relevant.

If the model or headline mechanism changes, invalidate and rebuild the affected positioning paragraphs.
