# Reference-paper writing standard for analytical OM manuscripts

Use this standard to reproduce the level of argument organization, analytical exposition, and prose discipline of a strong journal-style analytical OM working paper. It is a reusable standard, not a fixed crowdfunding outline. Adapt section labels and proof placement to the paper's mechanism and the user's instructions.

## 1. The paper is a chain of questions, not a container of results

Before drafting, express the paper as a cumulative analytical sequence:

1. What operational phenomenon creates the decision problem?
2. What unresolved tension, instrument-choice puzzle, policy comparison, or decision-right question follows?
3. What benchmark removes that feature?
4. What changes when the first continuation value, information linkage, or market feature is added?
5. What qualitatively new behavior appears when the focal linkage, instrument, policy, or decision right is added?
6. Which comparative static reveals the mechanism most sharply?
7. Can equilibrium coexistence or selection undo the apparent benefit?
8. Which targeted relaxations establish that the headline mechanism is not an artifact?

Each main section should answer one question and end by motivating the next. Do not organize the paper as a sequence of unrelated propositions or robustness checks.

### Choose the analytical architecture

Use the smallest architecture that explains the verified results:

- **Mechanism progression:** benchmark, enriched environments, opposing forces, interior or hybrid behavior, comparative statics, reversals, and equilibrium selection.
- **Policy and instrument comparison:** practical policy, proposed policy, available instruments, decision owner, exogenous versus endogenous policy intensity, policy dominance, and institutional scope.
- **Joint-lever decomposition:** operational response, actor preference alignment, one lever fixed, joint optimization of information and monetary or operational levers, substitution/complementarity regimes, benchmark outcomes, and a targeted third-lever extension.
- **Combined architecture:** use only when a policy change introduces a new mechanism and both dimensions carry headline results.

Do not force an opposing-forces story onto a paper whose contribution is instrument substitution, decision-right allocation, or posterior-dependent preference alignment. Do not turn every instrument comparison into a separate mechanism if the choices are generated by the same active constraint.

## 2. Abstract contract

Use a structured analytical abstract when the target style permits it:

- **Problem definition:** identify the operational evolution or institutional feature, the focal decision, and the unresolved tension.
- **Methodology/results:** name the model class and endogenous choices; state two to four linked findings, including the most distinctive regime, instrument ranking, reversal, decision-right result, or boundary; identify the mechanism rather than only the sign.
- **Managerial implications:** name the decision maker, the design lever, the direction of action, the stakeholder trade-off or win-win condition, and the boundary requiring caution.

Write the abstract after the paper is stable. Every substantive abstract claim must map to a formal result or clearly identified model implication. Use qualitative threshold descriptions unless a formula is itself the contribution.

## 3. Introduction architecture

Build the introduction with the following moves, normally in continuous prose:

1. **Institutional evolution.** Open with how the market, technology, or policy has changed the operational problem. Use one concrete, verified example when it materially clarifies the new linkage.
2. **Analytical puzzle.** Explain the unresolved tension, why existing instruments may fail, or why policy design and decision ownership are nontrivial.
3. **Research-question ladder.** State one focal question or a short sequence in which each subquestion motivates the next: existence, optimal instrument, policy design, decision ownership, and boundary conditions. Do not enumerate questions that the analysis does not separately answer.
4. **Model.** Describe actors, private/public information, timing, policies, and endogenous decisions at a level that lets readers anticipate the analysis.
5. **Finding groups.** Present two to four connected sets of results. For each group, state the benchmark, the changed equilibrium behavior, and the economic driver. Reserve the strongest detail for the distinctive regime, instrument ranking, policy comparison, reversal, or selection result.
6. **Closest-mechanism contrast.** When a result resembles prior work, explain why the causal force is different and why that difference matters for the equilibrium.
7. **Integrated implication.** Synthesize the mechanism into the actual design or governance question. State when a platform or firm should retain control, delegate a decision, mandate adoption, or allow choice only when the model supports that conclusion.
8. **Roadmap.** Make the roadmap mirror the analytical sequence.

The introduction should let a reader answer: What changed in practice? What is unresolved? What does the model endogenize? What are the two to four main result groups? Why are they not already implied by the closest paper? What should each modeled decision maker learn?

## 4. Literature-review architecture

Use two to four streams, chosen by intellectual role rather than keyword overlap. A useful ordering is:

- theoretical mechanism or signal/contract foundation;
- operational decisions in the focal institution;
- downstream market, continuation value, or adjacent process linked by the paper.

Within each stream:

1. Define the stream's central decision problem.
2. Group papers by mechanism or endogenous decision, not one sentence per citation.
3. Identify the closest paper or small cluster.
4. State the focal paper's exact structural difference.
5. Explain why that difference changes incentives, feasible actions, or equilibrium outcomes.

For the nearest structural neighbor, test whether the focal model nests it. If a parameter value, policy restriction, or removed decision recovers the prior model, state the recovery and identify exactly where the new result begins. If nesting is impossible, do not claim generalization; explain the nonnested comparison.

End with a cross-stream synthesis explaining what becomes visible only when the streams are connected. Do not claim that streams are “studied in isolation” unless the source-backed search supports that wording.

## 5. Model exposition

Move from institution to mathematics in this order:

1. Map the real process and the maintained scope.
2. State the timeline and what is observed at each move.
3. Define actors and type, demand, or information uncertainty.
4. Define endogenous decisions, their owners, and the available instruments; show how operational variables relate to monetary or probabilistic outcomes.
5. Introduce post-decision or continuation payoffs only after the reader understands the base stage.
6. Write objectives and constraints, with the owner of every variable explicit.
7. Explain any reparameterization and what economic meaning it preserves.
8. Provide a notation table before the formal analysis when notation is dense.
9. State the equilibrium concept, beliefs, incentive constraints, and relevant deviations.
10. When policy design matters, record whether adoption is mandatory or voluntary, whether intensity is fixed or chosen, and whether the platform, firm, or consumer owns each choice.

For information design, additionally state the state, prior, sender, receiver, acquisition process, experiment set, commitment technology, signal, posterior, Bayes-plausibility condition, receiver best response, and sender interim payoff. Distinguish a test that generates information from a disclosure rule that garbles or releases it.

Separate inherited model structure from genuinely new features so readers can see what produces the contribution, but do not require literal headings such as “base setting following the literature.”

For each consequential assumption, state: the mathematical property it guarantees; the trivial, infeasible, or off-scope cases it excludes; evidence that the admissible parameter region is nonempty; its institutional rationale; and the extension or limiting case that probes it. Normalizations need only a concise explanation.

## 6. Analysis architecture

Prefer cumulative environments when they isolate the mechanism:

- complete-information benchmark;
- asymmetric-information benchmark without the focal continuation or linkage;
- closest-paper recovery or policy-off benchmark when the contribution claims nesting;
- first enriched environment;
- main environment with the focal endogenous linkage;
- instrument or policy comparison and decision-right allocation when central;
- comparative statics and equilibrium selection;
- targeted extensions.

For joint information and monetary or operational levers, first characterize the receiver's action and the actors' posterior-dependent preferences. Use a fixed-lever problem only when it isolates the information mechanism, then endogenize the levers jointly and compare the resulting policy path with the no-information and first-best benchmarks.

For every transition, state what changes in the payoff, constraint, or information structure and what remains unchanged. The main environment should not be a renamed repetition of the benchmark.

When endogenizing a policy intensity or transferring its control to another actor, use a delegation gate: first establish feasibility and equilibrium existence with the parameter fixed, then determine whether allowing the actor to choose it preserves the intended outcome. Do not infer that more discretion is beneficial merely because it enlarges a feasible set.

Define complicated thresholds immediately before the proposition that uses them. Give explicit expressions in an appendix when they obscure the economics. Formal statements should partition regimes clearly and name the equilibrium design or outcome in each region.

## 7. Exposition after a formal result

After a central proposition, the discussion should usually perform these moves without rigid subheadings:

1. Translate the result into decisions and equilibrium regimes.
2. Compare it with the complete-information or preceding-environment benchmark.
3. Explain why the relevant incentive or feasibility constraint binds.
4. Decompose opposing forces or distinct sources of strategic power and identify which dominates in each region.
5. If the optimum is geometric, explain the constraint boundary, feasible region, isoprofit or indifference objects, and the selected point.
6. Name the regime in economically meaningful language and keep that label stable thereafter.
7. Contrast the mechanism with the closest superficially similar result in the literature.
8. If readers may regard a new instrument as equivalent to an old one, state the strongest equivalence objection and rebut it through different feasible sets, active constraints, equilibrium outcomes, or comparative statics.
9. State the decision implication or use the result to motivate the next comparative static.

Use more than a one-paragraph paraphrase for a headline proposition. Conversely, do not restate every mathematical clause if it has no separate economic meaning.

### Formal-result hierarchy

- Use lemmas for benchmarks, component characterizations, technical properties, or inherited results needed downstream.
- Use propositions for main equilibrium characterizations, instrument or policy rankings, reversals, and decision-right results.
- Use corollaries for transparent comparative statics, benchmark comparisons, or implications that follow directly from established results.
- Do not split one economic result across labels merely to increase the proposition count, and do not hide a headline result in a lemma.
- Mark a result as new, adapted, specialized, or inherited when it reproduces or modifies a formal result from the closest paper. Attribute inherited results explicitly and do not present them as contributions.

## 8. Comparative statics and reversals

For a comparative-static result:

- identify the truthful or benchmark behavior against which a deviation is evaluated;
- distinguish within-regime movement from jumps caused by switching regimes;
- separate direct payoff effects, strategic incentive effects, and equilibrium-selection effects;
- explain any reversal by locating the threshold at which the relevant ranking changes;
- state whether a result is local, global, weak, strict, continuous, or discontinuous;
- use a figure only when it clarifies regime changes, geometry, or nonmonotonicity.
- verify that any derived informativeness, efficiency, alignment, or elasticity measure is meaningful and differentiable on the claimed region; describe discontinuous policy switches as jumps rather than ordinary marginal effects.

When a parameter affects two types or two stages differently, analyze the channels separately before synthesizing the double-edged effect.

## 9. Pooling, coexistence, and selection

If the model permits multiple equilibrium forms, do not stop after characterizing separation or the preferred candidate. State:

- existence conditions for each equilibrium form;
- how the focal feature relaxes or tightens those conditions relative to the benchmark;
- the selection criterion and the parameter that drives selection;
- why a feature that improves discipline can still reduce information revelation ex ante.

Equilibrium selection belongs in the main analytical story when it qualifies the headline design implication.

## 10. Extensions and conclusion

Choose extensions that each answer a named threat to the mechanism, policy, or recommended allocation of control. An extension may remove an auxiliary market, introduce heterogeneity, weaken perfect discipline, change the funding or operating institution, or add execution uncertainty. Open the section by stating why each relaxation is diagnostic. After each extension, report whether the mechanism persists, attenuates, reverses, or is replaced and whether the feasible policy or decision owner changes.

If an extension requires numerical optimization, identify the analytically characterized frontier, the source of intractability, the regimes and boundaries searched, and the scope of the numerical claim. Do not convert a representative parameterization into a general robustness statement.

## 11. Stakeholder and policy claims

Before claiming that one policy dominates another, compare the modeled outcomes for every stakeholder named in the claim. Maintain a ledger for firm or creator profit, consumer or backer surplus, platform objective when modeled, success or service probability, information revelation, and any explicit welfare measure.

- `Optimal` requires a named objective and feasible set.
- `Preferred equilibrium` requires a stated selection or stakeholder criterion.
- `Dominates` requires the comparison dimensions and parameter region.
- `Win-win` requires weak improvement for every named stakeholder and a strict improvement for at least one, with no omitted modeled loss that contradicts the phrase.

Translate formal policy results into implementation details only when the model identifies the actor, observable condition, controlled variable, and direction. Distinguish a modeled prescription from an illustrative implementation suggestion.

The conclusion should retrace the research question, benchmark-to-main progression, distinctive mechanism or instrument ranking, reversal or selection result, decision-right boundary when relevant, and bounded managerial implication. Add no new theorem, citation-dependent claim, or policy prescription.

## 12. Prose standard

- Use stable terminology for actors, regimes, instruments, and environments.
- Give paragraphs explicit topic sentences; each paragraph should have one dominant claim.
- Use contrast and causal connectors only when the stated relation is real: “by contrast,” “because,” “consequently,” “thus,” and “taken together.”
- Prefer precise comparative language over promotional adjectives.
- Explain formulas through economic objects: success probability, continuation value, per-unit revenue, opportunity cost, imitation incentive, or active constraint.
- Keep formulas out of prose when a named threshold or qualitative condition is sufficient.
- Use “we show” for demonstrated results, “we find” for characterized outcomes, and “the model suggests” for bounded implications.
- Avoid sentence-level repetition, generic claims of novelty, inflated welfare language, and transitions that merely announce the next section.

### Reader momentum and controlled interest

For analytical OM writing, rigor and reader interest are complements. The paper should make its formal contribution easy to follow and make the reader want to follow it. Create that momentum through model-supported structure rather than promotional language.

- Open a main section or result group with the operational question, trade-off, or unresolved comparison that the analysis answers. Do not make a wall of thresholds the reader's first encounter with the result.
- For each headline result, establish the benchmark intuition before presenting a reversal, discontinuity, hybrid regime, or selection effect. Interest comes from the distance between the benchmark and the verified outcome.
- Preserve concise intuition paragraphs that translate an active constraint or marginal trade-off into economic objects. A proof summary is not a substitute for a mechanism explanation.
- Use concrete institutional details selectively in the introduction and implications when they clarify the modeled linkage. Verify them, and do not let examples substitute for general results.
- Vary analytical density. A short, accurate payoff sentence may follow a longer mechanism paragraph, especially when it names the reversal or the next question.
- End each result group by stating the unresolved boundary, competing force, or equilibrium question that motivates the next group. The transition should advance the analytical chain, not merely announce a section.
- Treat concision as the removal of repetition, vague modifiers, and unnecessary notation from prose. Do not remove stakes, comparison baselines, necessary antecedents, or causal bridges solely to make the manuscript shorter.
- Use memorable synthesis only when every term is supported by the model. Prefer a specific contrast such as “discipline imitation but raise the cost of separation” to generic claims that a result is novel, important, or counterintuitive.

## 13. Quality test

Before release, a reader should be able to recover the paper's entire logic from five aligned objects:

1. the abstract's result groups;
2. the introduction's finding sequence;
3. the section order;
4. the proposition and corollary chain;
5. the conclusion's synthesis.

For a policy or governance paper, a sixth object must also align: the policy-instrument-decision-rights and stakeholder-outcome map.

If these objects use different groupings, vocabulary, or causal explanations, revise the architecture rather than polishing sentences locally.
