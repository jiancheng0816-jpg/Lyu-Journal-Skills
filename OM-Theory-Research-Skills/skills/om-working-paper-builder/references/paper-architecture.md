# Analytical OM paper architecture

Adapt the architecture to the paper's mechanism; do not force sections that have no analytical role.

## Title and abstract

- Make the title name the decision problem or tension, not every model feature.
- When the target style permits, structure the abstract as `Problem definition`, `Methodology/results`, and `Managerial implications`.
- State the setting, research question, method, two to four linked verified findings, mechanism or instrument ranking, and implication in the abstract.
- Avoid literature claims, causal language, or generality beyond the evidence.

## Introduction moves

1. Establish the operational phenomenon and why an important decision is difficult.
2. Identify the unresolved trade-off, strategic tension, instrument-choice puzzle, policy comparison, or information friction.
3. State one focal research question or a connected question ladder precisely.
4. Explain the model's essential actors, timing, and endogenous choices without reproducing the model section.
5. Present the headline results as two to four groups that map exactly to the main analysis.
6. For each group, state the benchmark comparison, equilibrium change, and mechanism; give extra space to the distinctive regime, reversal, or selection result.
7. State contributions relative to named closest streams or papers, including why a superficially similar result arises from a different mechanism.
8. Synthesize the mechanism into a bounded managerial, platform-design, or decision-right implication and preview the paper.

## Literature review

- Organize by conversations and mechanisms, not an author-by-author list.
- End each stream with a precise relationship to the focal model.
- Give the closest paper or small cluster a mechanism-level comparison rather than burying it in a citation list.
- Distinguish foundations, closest neighbors, and contextual evidence.
- Carry evidence-status limits into the prose.
- End the section with a cross-stream synthesis explaining why connecting the streams changes the question or result.

## Model and analysis

- Open with institutional mapping and the event timeline.
- Define players, information, decisions, payoffs, constraints, equilibrium, and notation.
- Motivate assumptions that drive results; identify normalizations and scope restrictions.
- For consequential assumptions, explain what they guarantee and exclude, demonstrate a nonempty admissible region, and identify the relaxation that probes them.
- Use a benchmark to remove the focal mechanism or fix a key endogenous decision.
- Recover a claimed nested closest model under the relevant parameter or policy restriction.
- Order main results so each creates the next question: equilibrium characterization, mechanism or geometry, comparative statics, then coexistence or selection when relevant.
- Make the dependency between neighboring results explicit. After each proposition or corollary, explain what it adds relative to the previous benchmark, whether it is a new mechanism or a reverse policy mapping, and which remaining question motivates the next result.
- Give every formal result an interpretive discussion. Headline results normally require one or two paragraphs that translate the statement, explain the active mechanism, connect it to an institutional or managerial decision, and compare it with verified prior work when the directional conclusion materially agrees or conflicts.
- In sequential environments, identify the exact new payoff, constraint, or information linkage at each transition.
- Define complicated thresholds immediately before the result that uses them; move expressions that obscure the economics to the appendix.
- Put technical variants in extensions only when they test a mechanism boundary.
- When policy matters, distinguish mandatory versus voluntary adoption, fixed versus endogenous intensity, decision owner, instrument set, and stakeholder outcomes.
- For information-design papers, define the state-prior-experiment-posterior-action chain and derive a preference-alignment map before the optimal experiment.
- When information interacts with pricing, subsidy, contracting, capacity, or another lever, isolate each lever only long enough to expose its mechanism, then solve and narrate the joint policy.
- Use separate no-information, fixed-lever, full-information, first-best, and closest-paper benchmarks only when each performs a named analytical job.
- When closed forms end, state the analytical frontier and use a documented regime-by-regime numerical comparison rather than an unexplained parameter illustration.
- Place visuals inside the result chain rather than as detached summaries. Before each figure, identify the propositions and axis-to-primitive mapping it displays; after it, explain the panel-by-panel contrast, thresholds, discontinuities, and stakeholder or profit consequences. When two figures correspond to different institutions, state the cross-figure comparison explicitly.

## Managerial implications and conclusion

- Tie each implication to a comparative static or regime choice.
- Identify who should act, under what observable conditions, and in which direction.
- Distinguish prescriptions from interpretations.
- Audit `optimal`, `preferred`, `dominates`, and `win-win` against the named objective, criterion, stakeholders, and parameter scope.
- Synthesize implications after a coherent result group; do not force a generic recommendation after every proposition.
- Conclude with the mechanism, boundary conditions, and research opportunities; do not introduce new results.

## Appendix

Include detailed proofs, omitted equilibrium regions, robustness derivations, computational procedures, supplementary figures and tables, and notation when their placement improves the main text. Keep every appendix item linked from the main text.
