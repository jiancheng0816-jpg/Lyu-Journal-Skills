# Review triage and revision strategy

Use this protocol before drafting polished prose. The objective is to turn a collection of comments into one coherent scientific revision rather than a patchwork of local edits.

## 1. Reconstruct the editorial signal

Record the decision, recommendation from each reviewer, and the editor's synthesis. Distinguish:

- issues explicitly designated as publication conditions;
- issues shared by multiple reviewers;
- comments that threaten correctness or internal consistency;
- requests that improve the paper but are not central to the decision;
- minor exposition and presentation corrections.

For later rounds, also distinguish issues the review team declares closed, residual concerns from the prior round, reopened issues caused by the revision, and genuinely new comments. Read [multi-round-closure-protocol.md](multi-round-closure-protocol.md) when the current decision depends on one remaining hurdle.

A risky major revision requires more than many local fixes. Identify the smallest set of structural changes that would change the review team's assessment of validity, contribution, and implementability.

## 2. Build a cross-reviewer issue map

Cluster comments by root issue, even when reviewers phrase them differently:

| Root issue | Typical surface comments | Scientific question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Internal consistency | “How can the platform identify type?” | Can the stated mechanism be implemented under the information structure? |
| Institutional fit | “The platform uses another funding scheme” | Does the modeled institution correspond to the motivating practice? |
| Mechanism motivation | “This policy is not observed” | Is it descriptive, proposed, or merely analytical? What would implementation require? |
| Assumption dependence | “Consumers are homogeneous” | Which result is mechanical, and which mechanism survives relaxation? |
| Omitted force | refunds, moral hazard, production failure | Is the omitted force inactive, reinforcing, or overturning in the baseline? |
| Contribution | “This adds one feature to prior work” | What new decision, mechanism, boundary, or policy comparison exists? |
| Analysis | missing belief/refinement/sensitivity | Is the equilibrium complete, selected, and economically interpreted? |

Where comments overlap, design one manuscript change that resolves the root issue and tailor its explanation to each recipient.

## 3. Rank by scientific risk

Use four levels:

1. **Critical:** incorrect result, infeasible mechanism, contradictory information structure, or unsupported headline contribution.
2. **Structural:** wrong base model, missing central comparison, institutional mismatch, or major literature-positioning failure.
3. **Strengthening:** robustness, sensitivity, implementation discussion, additional intuition, or improved organization.
4. **Editorial:** notation, typos, citations, labels, and local clarity.

Resolve critical and structural issues before spending effort on response prose. In a later round, a single unresolved critical or structural issue can control the decision even when most reviewers recommend minor revision.

## 4. Select the revision action

For each issue, compare candidate actions by four criteria:

- Does it restore scientific validity?
- Does it answer the root concern rather than only its wording?
- Does it preserve or sharpen the paper's central question?
- Can the resulting claim be verified and explained cleanly?

Typical high-value moves include:

- deleting an infeasible mechanism rather than defending it rhetorically;
- replacing a convenient base case with the more general, credible model;
- treating a platform-wide policy parameter as exogenous in the main model and moving personalization to an extension;
- distinguishing an observed mechanism from a proposed mechanism;
- adding the alternative institutional regime that makes the challenged mechanism economically active;
- explaining why an omitted force is inactive in one regime but active in another;
- adding the minimum robustness test that targets the reviewer's causal concern;
- removing an extension whose behavioral foundation cannot be defended;
- repositioning the contribution around a new instrument, decision right, or regime comparison rather than a new context.
- promoting an endogenous or institutionally natural decision to the main model while moving the former imposed case to an appendix;
- replacing an exhaustive comparative-static proposition with its managerial shape, stakeholder trade-off, or finite candidate set;
- renaming constructs so labels identify economic content and decision ownership rather than chronology or vague information asymmetry;
- replacing an `if the policy works` contribution with a `how, when, and who controls it` contribution when the broad intuition is already known.

## 5. Protect the main paper

Do not answer every concern by enlarging the model. Decide whether an issue belongs in:

- the base model because it affects internal validity or the central mechanism;
- a main comparison because it creates a headline boundary or reversal;
- an extension because it tests a threatened assumption;
- a supplement because it is technically useful but narratively secondary;
- a limitations paragraph because it is outside the paper's chosen scope.

An extension is persuasive only if it states what changes, what survives, and why that resolves the targeted concern.

Reverify institutional facts when review rounds are separated in time. A change in platform policy may alter whether a regime belongs in the main application, an extension, or only historical motivation.

## 6. Reposition after structural revision

When the model changes substantially, rewrite the contribution rather than retaining the original pitch. Rebuild the literature comparison around:

- actors and information structure;
- endogenous decisions and decision rights;
- mechanism or instrument;
- funding, market, or policy regime;
- equilibrium and selection concept;
- affected stakeholder outcomes;
- exact nested benchmark, if one is claimed.

Contextual novelty is supporting evidence, not the main analytical contribution.

## 7. Define the stopping rule

The revision plan is ready when every critical and structural issue has one coherent disposition, required new analysis is identified, contradictions across reviewers are resolved, and the revised paper has a clearer central contribution than the original.
